Sensor fusion explained with two uncertain classroom measurements

Sensor fusion combines information with different strengths, uncertainties and timing to estimate a state. It is not simply averaging every available number. A weighted classroom example can show why confidence matters, while real drone estimators also model motion, sensor errors and consistency. The classroom arithmetic is an analogy, not a flight-stack implementation.

Ask why the measurements differ

Two observations of the same location may disagree because of noise, bias, timing or different reference frames. Before fusing anything, check whether they describe the same physical quantity. Combining an altitude above home with one above a different reference does not produce a better estimate merely because a formula was applied. Definitions come before numerical weighting.

Work through a limited weighted example

Suppose two fictional measurements of a fixed point are 10 and 14 centimetres in the same frame. For illustration, give the first three times the weight of the second. The weighted estimate is 11 centimetres. The chosen weights are classroom assumptions, not computed sensor uncertainties. Ask what evidence would justify them rather than presenting the result as automatically more accurate.

Prediction adds a second kind of information

A system with a motion model can predict a next state and compare new observations with that prediction. ArduPilot’s EKF overview explains the estimator’s relationship to inertial and other measurements. Students can demonstrate the idea with a dot moving one square per time step, then compare a noisy observation with the predicted position. The model can also be wrong.

Disagreement should sometimes trigger investigation

A new reading far from the prediction may represent actual motion, sensor trouble or a reference mismatch. Blindly averaging it can hide the conflict. Ask teams to record possible causes and the extra information needed. Real estimators use documented checks and error models; this article does not recommend changing those parameters or copying thresholds between vehicles.

Explain uncertainty instead of promising certainty

More sensors do not guarantee better results if they share a problem or are interpreted incorrectly. A useful report states what was combined, why the inputs were considered comparable and how uncertainty affected the conclusion. This reasoning is valuable even without deriving a Kalman filter, and it prepares students to read estimator status with appropriate care.

  • Match physical quantities, frames and timestamps.
  • Justify confidence rather than inventing it silently.
  • Keep prediction separate from observation.
  • Investigate conflicts before averaging them away.

Compare equal and unequal weighting

  1. Use fictional measurements of 10 and 14 centimetres for the same marked point.
  2. Compute an equal average and a three-to-one weighted estimate, explaining the assumptions behind each.
  3. Add a deliberately inconsistent observation of 40 centimetres and list competing explanations before calculating anything.
  4. Write what a real estimator would need beyond your two-number arithmetic, including timing and an error model.

Questions

Is sensor fusion just averaging GNSS and accelerometer values?

No. Those quantities have different meanings and require models, timing and coordinate handling. Real estimators are more structured than a simple average.

Does adding another sensor always improve accuracy?

No. Poor data, shared errors, timing problems and incorrect configuration can undermine an estimate. Additional information needs appropriate interpretation and validation.
