Find a drone model’s centre of mass with a simple worksheet

By YANAPublished 27 October 20263 min read

In this guide

  2. Work through a fictional calculation
  3. Moving mass can matter more than adding symmetry
  4. Extend the idea into three dimensions
  5. Compare calculation with a simple model
  6. Practical activity
  7. Questions
  8. References

The centre of mass depends on both component masses and their positions. For discrete items along one axis, multiply each mass by its position, add those moments and divide by total mass. Use a defined origin and consistent units. This explains layout trade-offs without claiming a payload limit for an aircraft.

Choose an origin before measuring

Place the centre of a paper frame at zero and define positive positions towards its front. A battery behind that point has a negative coordinate, not a negative mass. Add a second perpendicular axis when considering left-right balance. NASA’s centre-of-gravity explanation provides the physical basis, while a component worksheet makes the idea manageable for a classroom.

Work through a fictional calculation

Suppose a simplified board model has a 400 gram central structure at zero, a 200 gram block at plus 60 millimetres and a 100 gram block at minus 30 millimetres. The total is 700 grams and the summed first moment is 9,000 gram-millimetres. Dividing gives about plus 12.9 millimetres. These numbers describe a teaching model, not YANA hardware.

Moving mass can matter more than adding symmetry

The central structure may look symmetric while a heavy accessory shifts the mass distribution. Move the 200 gram block closer to the origin and repeat the calculation. Ask students to explain why relocating a heavy item can have more effect than relocating a light item by the same distance. A neat visual arrangement is not a substitute for a mass-position table.

Extend the idea into three dimensions

Height matters too. A top-mounted item and an under-mounted item may share horizontal coordinates while having different vertical coordinates. Use stacked cardboard layers to represent this difference. The worksheet can apply the same weighted-average method independently to each axis. Avoid treating the top-view centre mark as a complete three-dimensional description of the assembly.

Compare calculation with a simple model

A classroom model made from known blocks can help students check their arithmetic using a supervised balance demonstration. Do not balance a connected aircraft on sharp points or improvise tests with a battery attached. Record measurement uncertainty and omitted items such as cables. The educational result is an explained mass distribution, not proof of dynamic stability or operational readiness.

  • Define the origin and positive axes.
  • Include every model component mass.
  • Keep mass and length units consistent.
  • State omitted items and uncertainty.

Move one block and predict the balance point

  1. Use cardboard and labelled classroom masses with the fictional values in the example.
  2. Calculate the initial position, then predict the effect of moving the 200 gram block from plus 60 to plus 20 millimetres.
  3. Check the new arithmetic and compare the direction of change with your prediction.
  4. Add a small omitted-item allowance and discuss how missing cables could change a real layout estimate.

Questions

Is the geometric centre always the centre of mass?

No. It is only the mass centre when the distribution supports that conclusion. Unequal component masses and positions can move it away from the geometric centre.

Does a centred mass distribution prove a drone will fly correctly?

No. It addresses one mechanical property. Propulsion, configuration, sensing, structure and operating conditions still require separate assessment.
