Why a resting accelerometer does not simply read zero

By YANAPublished 27 October 20263 min read

An accelerometer’s reading is not simply the aircraft’s position or its everyday acceleration through the room. A supported stationary sensor typically shows a gravity-related response along its axes. Orientation and motion affect interpretation, so students should distinguish raw sensor fields from gravity-compensated or estimated acceleration in software.

Begin with the field definition

A plotted field called acceleration may be a raw measurement, a scaled measurement or a processed estimate. Those stages are not interchangeable. MAVLink’s message definitions specify units and field meanings, while flight-stack documentation explains processing. Before interpreting a trace, write the exact message or log field rather than relying on a friendly dashboard title.

A stationary orientation changes the axis components

Consider a paper sensor with three arrows. When one axis is vertical, the gravity-related supported response appears mainly along that axis in a simple model. Rotate the sensor and its components redistribute. The physical room has not acquired a new acceleration merely because the sensor was turned. Signs depend on the actual frame and message convention, so do not assume them.

Tilt and translation can be confused in a simple reading

An accelerometer alone does not immediately explain whether a changed component came from tilt, translational motion or both. That ambiguity motivates combining information in state estimation. Students can draw two different physical scenarios that produce similar one-axis values. The exercise teaches that one number may be consistent with several explanations rather than uniquely identifying a movement.

Use calibrated interpretation rather than a visual guess

PX4 identifies accelerometer data as part of velocity estimation and links to calibration. ArduPilot documents calibration of offsets and axis-related effects. The details depend on the actual stack and board. Introductory classroom work can use supplied datasets, leaving real calibration to the instructor’s verified process with propellers removed and hardware handled according to its documentation.

Keep the conclusion narrower than the model

A three-axis paper model helps explain orientation-dependent components; it does not reproduce every sensor imperfection or estimator step. Label the assumed frame, scale and processing stage. A good student report can explain why a stationary magnitude need not be zero while acknowledging that the sign and exact value require the real field definition and conditions.

  • Record the exact field and processing stage.
  • Draw sensor axes and the vertical direction.
  • Separate tilt from translational hypotheses.
  • Check units and signs in the actual message definition.

Draw the gravity-related components

  1. Use a paper cube with three coloured axes and draw the classroom vertical direction beside it.
  2. Sketch the expected component distribution for two stationary orientations without claiming universal signs.
  3. Invent two possible explanations for a changed single-axis reading and identify an extra observation that could distinguish them.
  4. Write a field dictionary specifying whether your hypothetical numbers are raw, scaled or gravity-compensated.

Questions

Does a stationary accelerometer always show zero on every axis?

No. A supported sensor typically has a gravity-related response. Interpretation depends on orientation, field definition, sign convention and processing.

Can I obtain position by adding accelerometer values?

Not directly. Units, timing, orientation, gravity handling and errors matter, and position estimation requires more than an unqualified sum of readings.
