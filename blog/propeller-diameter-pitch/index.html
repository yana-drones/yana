Propeller diameter and pitch: read the geometry before the label

By YANAPublished 27 October 20263 min read

  2. Blade angle is not one universal number

  7. Questions

Propeller diameter describes its swept size, while pitch-related terms describe blade geometry or an idealised advance convention. A printed size code is not a guarantee of travel per revolution or thrust. Read the manufacturer’s definition and tested combinations, and keep classroom inspection disconnected from any powered propeller demonstration.

Diameter concerns the swept circle

Draw a rotor centre and a circle passing through the blade tips. Diameter spans that circle through its centre; radius is half the diameter. Students can compare paper circles before seeing any real hardware. Clearance must account for actual geometry and installation, but this drawing cannot establish acceptable aircraft spacing or justify fitting a larger propeller.

Blade angle is not one universal number

A blade’s geometry can vary along its span, so a single photograph or angle measurement is incomplete. NASA’s OpenVSP propeller tutorial discusses diameter, blade count and pitch-related modelling controls, illustrating that multiple geometric choices exist. A classroom paper blade can show changing angles at different stations without pretending to reproduce a real manufacturer’s aerodynamic design.

Naming conventions need a source

A size label may combine nominal diameter and pitch information, but the exact convention belongs to the manufacturer. Do not interpret every printed code by analogy with one familiar brand. Create a label dictionary with the maker’s source and stated units. If the source is unavailable, write unknown rather than assuming that a number is millimetres, inches or a blade count.

Geometry interacts with motor and supply

Changing propeller geometry changes the propulsion problem. A larger-looking blade is not automatically an efficiency improvement, and a shared diameter does not make two designs interchangeable. The relevant evidence includes compatible motor, voltage, current, thermal conditions and published test context. A component comparison should preserve those conditions rather than extracting a single headline thrust number.

Choose an inspection task with a clear limit

Use printed photographs or disconnected propellers handled under instructor guidance. Ask students to identify diameter, blade count, hub details and missing manufacturer information. Do not run an exposed rotor to demonstrate a label. The finished worksheet should explain the geometry and its uncertainties while leaving actual suitability to verified specifications and qualified review.

  • Distinguish radius from diameter.
  • Record the manufacturer’s label convention.
  • Keep geometry separate from performance claims.
  • Verify the complete tested propulsion combination.

Read three fictional propeller labels

  1. Draw three paper propellers with different diameters, blade counts and intentionally ambiguous labels.
  2. Ask teams to identify visible geometry and mark every label meaning that requires documentation.
  3. Provide a fictional manufacturer key for two labels and leave the third unresolved.
  4. Explain why two paper shapes with the same diameter cannot be assigned equal thrust without further evidence.

Questions

Does pitch tell me the exact distance a drone moves per revolution?

No. Propeller motion through air is not a rigid screw moving through a solid material. Geometry alone does not establish actual advance or vehicle travel.

Can a larger propeller be assumed to improve flight time?

No. Compatibility, current, mass, geometry and operating conditions all matter. Use verified data for the complete combination.
