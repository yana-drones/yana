Showcase student drone work without overstating results

By YANAPublished 27 October 20263 min read

In this guide

  1. Choose one story per display
  2. Use original evidence carefully
  3. Distinguish result and aspiration
  4. Example: a documentation showcase
  5. Plan interaction and follow-up
  6. Practical activity
  7. Questions
  8. References

Show the project question, actual artefact and evidence in language a newcomer can follow. Label prototype, recorded and simulated material accurately. Credit contributions with permission and avoid implying partnerships, certification or performance that the project has not established. A useful showcase explains what was learned and what remains unresolved.

Choose one story per display

A clear display answers what the team investigated, what it produced and what the result means. Avoid covering a poster with every acronym encountered during the project. Use a readable heading, one main diagram and a concise explanation. Provide a link to the maintained project record for visitors who want more detail rather than shrinking all the text to fit.

Use original evidence carefully

Identify whether an image shows the current prototype, a previous configuration or an illustrative edit. A background-removed photograph can aid readability, but it should not hide meaningful component differences. Obtain permission before publishing identifiable people or institutional names. Do not use a host logo to imply endorsement or partnership unless that use has actually been authorised.

Distinguish result and aspiration

Use separate labels for observed results and proposed next work. A simulated graph does not verify physical performance, and a flight clip does not establish every advertised specification. State conditions and limitations where relevant. If no figure has been verified, omit it rather than borrowing one from a similar aircraft or giving an attractive estimate.

Example: a documentation showcase

A team can display its original and revised system maps, the reader-feedback method and two specific improvements. The caption explains that the study evaluated diagram clarity rather than aircraft readiness. Visitors can understand the engineering contribution without a flying performance. The team can also show the remaining unresolved interface question as a useful next investigation rather than conceal it.

Plan interaction and follow-up

Give visitors a short question they can answer from the display, then offer a contact route for deeper discussion. Any live practical activity needs separate assessment and supervision. Cornell’s outcome-design guidance supports making the learning purpose visible; NASA’s design-process resource is relevant context for showing iteration. Keep a copy of the published description with the project so later publicity remains consistent with the evidence.

Test the display from the distance at which visitors will actually read it. A readable caption and a clear evidence label contribute more to understanding than a dense technical poster that requires someone to explain every line aloud.

  • Explain one question, artefact and result.
  • Label prototypes, edits and simulation accurately.
  • Obtain permission for people and institutional references.
  • Separate verified outcomes from proposed next work.

Review a showcase caption

  1. Circle every claim of capability or affiliation.
  2. Link each claim to actual evidence or permission.
  3. Rewrite the caption so a new visitor can understand the limitation.

Questions

Must a showcase include a live flight?

No. A readable explanation of a genuine engineering investigation can be valuable. Live operation introduces separate planning and permission questions.

Can edited product photos be used?

Yes, when accurate and appropriately labelled. Edits should not invent components or imply a finished configuration that has not been confirmed.
