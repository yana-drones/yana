Compare motor and propeller test data without losing the conditions

By YANAPublished 27 October 20263 min read

In this guide

  1. Keep one row per test condition
  2. Match units and definitions first
  3. Use a fictional efficiency comparison
  4. Maximum values may describe different moments
  5. Write a conclusion for the evidence available
  6. Practical activity
  7. Questions
  8. References

A propulsion comparison needs the complete tested combination and conditions, not isolated maximum figures. Record motor, propeller, supply, operating point, instrumentation and units together. Compare like conditions or explain the differences. Published bench data helps technical reasoning but cannot automatically establish whole-aircraft performance, safe limits or a YANA product specification.

Keep one row per test condition

A row should represent a defined observation rather than a product name alone. Include the actual propeller model, rotational speed, air conditions where available and the measured quantities. The University of Illinois propeller database is a primary source of experimental small-propeller measurements and explains its coefficient definitions. Read those definitions before comparing its plots with another source.

Match units and definitions first

A force in newtons and a mass-equivalent label in grams cannot be compared as bare numbers. Similarly, a dimensionless coefficient is not a measured force. Add unit and definition columns before entering results. If a source omits a necessary definition, flag the comparison as incomplete rather than silently choosing the interpretation that makes the data align.

Use a fictional efficiency comparison

Imagine two classroom rows measured under the same invented condition: A produces four newtons for 40 watts, and B produces five newtons for 60 watts. Their force-to-input-power ratios are 0.10 and about 0.083 newton per watt. A has the higher ratio at those points, while B produces more force. Neither statement means one is universally better across all operating conditions.

Maximum values may describe different moments

The largest thrust figure and best efficiency figure on a sheet may come from different operating points. Combining them creates a fictional performance claim. Ask students to underline which columns must stay attached to the same row. A graph can help reveal trade-offs, but only when its axes, conditions and available operating range remain clear.

Write a conclusion for the evidence available

A sound report states the conditions under which one ratio or value was higher, then identifies what the data does not establish. It should not infer whole-aircraft endurance, payload or thermal suitability from one bench row. Keep calculations labelled as classroom examples and avoid promoting an unconfirmed motor-propeller combination as included with YANA’s educational quadcopter.

  • Retain the complete tested combination.
  • Match units and coefficient definitions.
  • Compare the same operating condition.
  • Do not combine maxima from different rows.

Choose a comparison question before ranking

  1. Create the two fictional rows from the example and add a third row at a different operating condition.
  2. Ask one team to compare force and another to compare force per input watt.
  3. Have both explain why their conclusions answer different questions.
  4. Mark the unmatched third row and list what would be needed for a fair additional comparison.

Questions

Does a published thrust table prove the flight time of a drone?

No. Aircraft mass, system power, usable energy, installation and operating conditions are additional factors requiring suitable evidence.

Can I combine best efficiency and maximum thrust from different rows?

No. Those values may describe different operating points. Preserve each result with its own conditions.

References and further reading
