Cable routing for educational drones: clarity, strain and inspection

By YANAPublished 27 October 20263 min read

In this guide

  1. Give every cable a documented purpose
  2. Plan for strain rather than appearance alone
  3. Electrical layout has mechanical consequences
  4. Design for removal and inspection
  5. Create a route review record
  6. Practical activity
  7. Questions
  8. References

Good cable routing keeps connections identifiable, supported and inspectable while respecting electrical and mechanical requirements. A short-looking route is not automatically the best route. Use the exact component guidance for wire ratings, interfaces and separation; classroom layout work should use disconnected hardware or string rather than experimental live wiring.

Give every cable a documented purpose

A route drawing should identify both endpoints and the cable’s function. Distinguish power, signal and combined harnesses instead of colouring all lines alike. Add the relevant connector and pinout document to the inventory. A cable that physically reaches a socket can still be electrically incompatible, so route planning cannot replace a separate interface check.

Plan for strain rather than appearance alone

A taut cable can pull on a connector or bridge a flexible mount. A loose unsupported loop can be difficult to inspect or retain. Use string on a printed frame plan to compare routes, leaving space for documented connector access and appropriate restraint. Do not infer minimum bend radius or acceptable tie pressure without the cable manufacturer’s guidance.

Electrical layout has mechanical consequences

ArduPilot’s ESC grounding discussion explains that wiring arrangement can influence signalling and electrical noise. The classroom lesson is that geometry has electrical meaning; it is not a licence to improvise a power harness. Separate a concept drawing from an approved wiring plan, and have actual wire sizing, protection and connections reviewed by a qualified instructor.

Design for removal and inspection

Trace the steps needed to remove one component without pulling on unrelated connectors. If every cable must be cut free to inspect the controller, the installation creates maintenance work. Labels should remain readable from normal inspection positions. A service loop may help access, but its suitability still depends on restraint, separation and the actual mechanical environment.

Create a route review record

Photograph the disconnected layout from above and the side, mark the front and label hidden endpoints on the drawing. Record which routes are proposals and which have been verified against component documentation. A clean drawing helps subsequent classes understand the assembly and lets an instructor find undocumented changes. Visual neatness is one outcome, not the acceptance criterion.

  • Identify both endpoints and cable purpose.
  • Check strain and mount bypasses.
  • Preserve connector access and readable labels.
  • Keep routing proposals separate from verified wiring.

Route string on a paper frame

  1. Print a top-view frame plan and add paper blocks for major components.
  2. Give teams three colours of string for different cable functions and ask for two possible layouts.
  3. Ask another student to remove one paper component by following the proposed access path.
  4. Annotate strain points, unclear endpoints and questions that require electrical documentation before a physical harness could be accepted.

Questions

Can cable colour tell me its exact electrical function?

Not reliably. Colours can help a documented harness, but verify endpoints, pinout and manufacturer information before assuming polarity or signal type.

Is the shortest route always best?

No. Access, strain, mechanical restraint, electrical behaviour and manufacturer requirements may justify a different route.
