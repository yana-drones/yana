Sampling and aliasing: why a slow plot can hide fast vibration

By YANAPublished 27 October 20263 min read

  2. Distinguish the processing stages
  3. Use the sampling limit as a concept
  4. Different sample schedules can suggest different motion

  6. Practical activity
  7. Questions
  8. References

Sampling records a changing signal at selected times. If a signal changes too quickly relative to those samples, the recorded pattern can misrepresent the motion through aliasing. Display refresh, logged sample rate and sensor sampling are different stages. Understand those stages before inferring physical vibration from a slowly updated graph.

A snapshot does not contain the whole motion

Imagine photographing a rotating arrow only once per turn. Every picture can show the same direction even though the arrow moved continuously between pictures. A classroom paper sequence illustrates why unchanged samples do not always mean unchanged physical state. The observation interval is part of the evidence and must be recorded with the sequence.

Distinguish the processing stages

A sensor can sample, software can filter or downsample, a logger can record and a display can refresh at different rates. The number of visible graph points does not identify every upstream rate. Draw these stages as separate boxes. Ask which documented rate governs the analysis rather than assuming the fastest number on a hardware specification describes the exported data.

Use the sampling limit as a concept

ArduPilot’s IMU batch-sampling documentation discusses frequency analysis and the relationship between sample rate and the frequencies that can be represented. An introductory lesson can state that signals approaching or exceeding half the sample rate require particular care and appropriate filtering assumptions. Do not choose operational filter settings from this conceptual rule alone; real systems involve additional implementation details.

Different sample schedules can suggest different motion

Prepare a paper rotating arrow that advances by a fixed angle each step. Observe every step, then every third step. The apparent sequence can change dramatically. Students should describe the complete underlying sequence and the observed subset separately. This is more instructive than merely saying a graph looks noisy, because it identifies what information was lost by the observation process.

Report a frequency claim with its limitations

A peak on a processed spectrum needs sample rate, window length, processing method and field identity. A slowly refreshed dashboard is not automatically evidence of low physical vibration. Keep raw data and processing metadata, and avoid attributing a peak to a particular component without corroboration. Classroom sequences can teach this reasoning without generating vibration on real equipment.

  • Record the actual analysed sample rate.
  • Separate sensing, logging and display refresh.
  • Identify downsampling and filtering steps.
  • State frequency-analysis limits beside conclusions.

Sample a paper rotating arrow

  1. Draw twelve arrow orientations representing equal time steps around repeated rotations.
  2. Ask one team to observe every drawing and another to observe only selected steps.
  3. Compare the apparent motion and explain how different observations can arise from the same underlying sequence.
  4. Draw a sensor-to-display pipeline and identify where rate information would be required for a real vibration analysis.

Questions

Does a smooth dashboard prove a smooth physical signal?

No. Filtering, downsampling and refresh rate can hide faster changes. Inspect the actual field and processing pipeline.

Can a sampled signal reveal any frequency if the plot is enlarged?

No. Enlarging a graph does not restore information absent from the sampling process. Interpretation depends on sampling and processing limits.
